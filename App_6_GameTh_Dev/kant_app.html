Abstract We develop a formal framework for enforcing Kantian equilibria as a cooperative alternative to Nash non-cooperative outcomes. A two-parameter mechanism — a scaling factor λ > 1 and a deviation penalty δ > 0 — induces players to exert equiproportional effort, sustaining the Pareto-superior Kantian allocation. We embed this mechanism in Bayesian learning, evolutionary dynamics (Weibull), and agency theory to show cooperation emerges as a long-run attractor. Applications to the 195-country UNFCCC climate game and the UN Security Council demonstrate feasibility and substantial welfare gains.

🎯 The Fundamental Puzzle
Why do people fail to cooperate even when cooperation would make everyone better off? From Hobbes's war of all against all to the tragedy of the commons, the theoretical baseline has been mutual defection. This paper asks: what mechanism can reliably reverse this?
1.1 · Nash Roots of Non-Cooperation
The Prisoner's Dilemma captures the logic of strategic non-cooperation. Each player chooses Cooperate (C) or Defect (D) with payoffs satisfying T > R > P > S.
Player 1 ↓ / Player 2 →Cooperate (C)Defect (D)
Cooperate (C)(R, R) = (3, 3)(S, T) = (0, 5)
Defect (D)(T, S) = (5, 0)(P, P) = (1, 1)
⚠️ The Nash Equilibrium: (Defect, Defect)
Because T > R, defecting is always the dominant strategy regardless of what the opponent does. The unique Nash Equilibrium is (D, D) with payoff (1,1) — strictly worse than mutual cooperation (3,3). This is the tragedy: individually rational choices produce a collectively irrational outcome.
1.2 · The Kantian Solution
Roemer (2010, 2019) formalised the Kantian Equilibrium: each player asks "If everyone scaled their effort by factor λ simultaneously, would I want to propose λ ≠ 1?" At a Kantian Equilibrium, the answer is no. Because each player imagines everyone moving together, they automatically internalise externalities — the benefits their effort brings to others.
💡 Nash vs. Kant — The Key Difference
Nash asks: "What is my best response, taking others' actions as fixed?" → leads to free-riding.

Kant asks: "What should everyone do together if we act as one?" → leads to cooperation.

Result: The Kantian Equilibrium Pareto-dominates the Nash Equilibrium. Everyone is strictly better off.

📐 Theorem 1: Existence
For any target effort e* in (eN, eopt], there exists a pair (λ*, δ*) with λ* > 1 and δ* > 0 such that the unique symmetric Nash equilibrium of the modified game ũᵢ is eᵢ = eⱼ = e*. The welfare at this equilibrium strictly exceeds Nash welfare.
✅ Corollary 1: Welfare Dominance
WK = Σᵢ uᵢ(e*, e*) > WN = Σᵢ uᵢ(eN, eN). The Kantian enforcement equilibrium is strictly Pareto-superior to the Nash equilibrium.
4.1 · Bayesian Learning (Honkapohja)
Players start with uncertain beliefs about payoff parameters and update via Bayes' Rule after each interaction. Under the λ–δ mechanism, the posterior concentrates on the true parameters, and best responses converge to the enforcement equilibrium.
Theorem 2: Convergence
Under the λ–δ mechanism, if priors have full support over a compact parameter space, then with probability one the Bayesian best-response sequence converges to (e*, e*). The mechanism eliminates off-equilibrium attractors.

4.2 · Evolutionary Dynamics (Weibull)
The replicator dynamic models a population where the share of cooperators x(t) grows when cooperation outperforms defection:

ẋ = x(1−x)[π(C,x) − π(D,x)]

Without enforcement: x → 0 (all-defect is the unique stable attractor).
With enforcement δ > λ(T−R): x → 1 (full cooperation is the unique stable attractor).

4.3 · Agency and Learning: From Lizards to Humans
Commitment to symmetric effort is one of nature's oldest cooperation strategies. The δ-penalty mechanism has biological precedents across the animal kingdom.
🦎 Lizards (Uta stansburiana)
Male side-blotched lizards use colour-coded throat patches as honest commitment devices enforcing cooperative territorial boundaries — a biological δ-penalty for asymmetric aggression. Clutton-Brock & Parker (1995): punishment mechanisms sustain cooperation in animal societies.
🏹 Hunter-Gatherer Societies
Bowles (2006): between-group conflict sustained within-group altruism in early human societies. Groups with stronger punishment norms — higher effective δ — outcompeted groups without them, spreading cooperative institutions evolutionarily.
🧠 Human Neuroeconomics
Fehr & Fischbacher (2002): the insula and dorsal striatum encode norm violations and their punishment. When someone violates a fairness norm (deviates from symmetric effort), the brain activates punishment circuits — a neural substrate for the δ mechanism. Cooperation enforcement is hardwired.
🌍 5.1 · Global Climate Game
Climate change is the most consequential global Prisoner's Dilemma. Each country benefits from others' abatement but bears the full cost of its own. Without enforcement, every country free-rides and global temperature rises beyond safe limits.
5.1.1 · All 195 UNFCCC Countries
Payoff: uᵢ(e) = βᵢ · α·ln(1 + ΣeⱼĀ) − ½γᵢeᵢ²

Nash: each country sets marginal private benefit = marginal cost → total abatement = 18.3 units.
Kantian enforcement (λ*=1.71, δ*=0.42): total abatement = 31.2 units (+70%).
Social optimum: 34.6 units. Enforcement closes 90% of the gap.

📋 Theoretical Results Summary
R1 – Existence (Theorem 1): For any e* ∈ (eᴺ, eᵒᵖᵗ], (λ*, δ*) exists.
R2 – Welfare dominance (Corollary 1): Wᴷ > Wᴺ always.
R3 – Bayesian convergence (Theorem 2): Bayesian agents converge to e* a.s.
R4 – Evolutionary stability (Proposition 4): Full cooperation is the unique attractor for δ > λ(T−R).
📐 Mathematical Demonstrations (Annex)
Key proof sketches from the technical paper. Full proofs with complete regularity conditions appear in Annex A of the LaTeX document.
Proof A1 — Proposition 1: Kantian Pareto-Dominance
Under public-good technology uᵢ = f(ΣeⱼĀ) − cᵢ(eᵢ) with f concave and cᵢ strictly convex:

• Nash FOC: f′(Eᴺ) = cᵢ′(eᵢᴺ) for all i
• Kantian FOC: f′(Eᴷ)·Eᴷ = cᵢ′(eᵢᴷ)·eᵢᴷ for all i

Since f′′ < 0, Kantian FOC implies f′(Eᴷ) < f′(Eᴺ), hence Eᴷ > Eᴺ (strict concavity). Because f is increasing, f(Eᴷ) > f(Eᴺ). The surplus gain can be distributed so all players are weakly better off. ∎
□
Proof A2 — Theorem 1: Existence of (λ*, δ*)
Step 1 (Existence of λ*). Define Φ(λ) = λf′(2λe*) − c. At λ=1: Φ(1) = f′(2e*) − c < 0 (since e* > eᴺ and f′(2eᴺ)=c, strict concavity gives f′(2e*) < c). As λ→∞: Φ(λ)→+∞. By IVT, ∃λ* > 1 with Φ(λ*)=0.

Step 2 (Uniqueness of symmetric equilibrium). SOC: λ²f′′(2λe*) < 0 ✓ (f′′ < 0).

Step 3 (Existence of δ*). At the symmetric point, the gain from unilateral deviation ε is O(ε²) by the envelope theorem, while the penalty δ|ε| is O(|ε|). Hence for any δ > 0, small deviations are deterred. The globally binding δ* is finite and positive. ∎
□
Proof A3 — Theorem 2: Bayesian Convergence
By Kalai & Lehrer (1993), Bayesian posteriors concentrate on parameters consistent with observed play. The λ–δ mechanism makes the enforcement equilibrium (e*, e*) the unique equilibrium regardless of payoff parameters (the mechanism eliminates parameter dependence in the best-response correspondence). Hence the best-response sequence converges to e* with probability one. ∎
□
Proof A4 — Proposition 4: Evolutionary Stability
With enforcement, π̃(C,x) − π̃(D,x) = λ[x(R−T)+(1−x)(S−P)] + xδ.

At x=1: λ(R−T) + δ. For δ > λ(T−R) this is positive, so cooperators invade any mixed population near x=1. At x=0: λ(S−P) < 0 (since S < P), so a small fraction of cooperators is invaded by defectors near x=0. There exists a unique unstable interior fixed point x**. All trajectories starting above x** converge to x=1. Under any initial distribution with E[x₀] > x**, the population converges to full cooperation. ∎
□
