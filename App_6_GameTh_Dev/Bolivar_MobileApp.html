The Liberator's Dilemma

Game theory, power & the independence of Latin America — from Simón Bolívar to García Márquez

Miguel Rocha de Sousa · Universidade de Évora

What is this paper?

Imagine playing chess — but the pieces are armies, colonial governors, creole landowners, and millions of people across an entire continent. The moves are battles, alliances, promises, and betrayals. The stakes are the freedom of six nations.

This paper takes Simón Bolívar's liberation of South America (1810–1826) and analyses it using game theory — the mathematical science of strategic decision-making.

"Bolívar solved the military game of liberation — and lost the political game of institution-building."
Two main inspirations
📖
El general en su laberinto
García Márquez, 1989
▾

García Márquez's Nobel-winning novel follows Bolívar's final journey down the Magdalena River in 1830 — terminally ill, politically defeated, watching his republic crumble. The paper reads this as a game-theoretic tragedy: a man who understood the strategic landscape perfectly but lacked the institutional tools to change the equilibrium.

📐
Economic Origins of Dictatorship & Democracy
Acemoglu & Robinson, 2005
▾

This landmark book asks: why do some countries become democracies and others dictatorships? Their answer: it comes down to strategic power games between elites (who benefit from the current system) and citizens (who might demand change). Democratisation happens when elites cannot credibly promise to maintain privileges without actually sharing power.

Key formula: Citizens revolt when expected payoff from revolution exceeds the status quo. Elites repress when the cost of granting power exceeds the threat. Everything else follows.
The big conclusions
1

Colonial regimes collapse when their commitment technology is destroyed. Napoleon's occupation was that shock.

2

Costly signals — not words — build coalitions. Bolívar won the llaneros through expensive, concrete commitments.

3

Post-independence failure was built-in. When the elites who made independence happen control the new states, extractive institutions persist.

4

Gran Colombia's collapse was mathematically inevitable. A political coalition with an empty core always breaks apart.

Bolívar's Campaigns

Bolívar did not liberate one country. He liberated six: Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia, and Panama — marching across the Andes in winter and through fever-ridden jungles for sixteen years.

Campaign Timeline
1810
Caracas Junta
Napoleon's occupation of Spain destroys royal legitimacy. Creoles seize the moment.
1811–1812
First Republic — and its Collapse
Patriots fail to coordinate. Regional rivalries select the inefficient equilibrium.
1813
Admirable Campaign
Bolívar marches from Cartagena to Caracas in a stunning military campaign.
1817–1818
Recruiting the Llaneros
Costly land grants and military promotions convert former enemies into the core of Bolívar's army.
1819
Andes Crossing & Boyacá
An irreversible winter crossing makes retreat impossible. Colombia is liberated.
1821
Carabobo — Venezuela Free
The decisive battle secures Venezuelan independence permanently.
1822
Guayaquil Meeting
Bolívar and San Martín meet secretly. San Martín withdraws. A Nash bargain resolves the command question.
1824
Junín & Ayacucho
Spain's last armies surrender. The liberation game reaches its terminal node.
1826–1830
Gran Colombia Collapses
The coalition's core is empty. Venezuela and Ecuador split. Bolívar descends the Magdalena River, dying.
The Nine Games

Each historical moment is modelled as a formal strategic game. Tap each card to explore what happened and why the math predicts it.

García Márquez

The paper argues that great literature often captures the same truths as formal economic models — expressed in human rather than mathematical language. Eight novels by García Márquez are woven into the analysis.

"García Márquez called this condition soledad — solitude. The isolation of societies unable to coordinate on the efficient equilibrium of inclusive institutions."

The Academic Paper

The full paper — The Liberator's Dilemma: Game Theory, Power, and the Independence of Latin America — models nine historical games using the framework of Acemoglu & Robinson (2005), includes a TikZ cartographic map of Bolívar's campaigns, game-theoretic diagrams and pgfplots figures, and a full BibTeX bibliography.

Produced as a LaTeX academic working paper for the Department of Economics, Universidade de Évora.
